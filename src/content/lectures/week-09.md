The last lecture before you fly, and the one with the least aerodynamics in
it.

An approach compresses time. The decisions it demands arrive in a fixed order
at increasing speed, and each is made with less margin than the one before,
which is the opposite of the condition under which people decide well. The
standard answer to this is to move the decision earlier — to make it on the
ground, in a form that requires only a check at the moment of use.

The lecture is mostly about why that answer, which everyone agrees with, fails
so reliably in practice.

The mechanism is not recklessness. It is that continuing a marginal approach
almost always works. Each time it works, the margin that was skipped is
retrospectively reclassified as margin that was not needed, and the standard
moves. Nobody makes a decision to lower it. It lowers, incrementally, through
a series of individually reasonable calls, until the accepted practice is one
that would have been rejected outright if proposed on day one.

This has a literature and a name, drawn from the analysis of organisational
failures well outside aviation. We will look at one case from a different
industry entirely, because the shape is easier to see when the technical
detail is unfamiliar.

Then the uncomfortable part: identifying, individually and out loud, which
gate each of you is going to rationalise past in week 10.
