The last lecture before you fly, and the one with the least aerodynamics
in it.

An approach compresses time. The decisions it demands arrive in a fixed
order at increasing speed, each with less margin than the one before —
the opposite of the condition under which people decide well. The standard
answer is to move the decision earlier: make it on the ground, in a form
that requires only a check at the moment of use.

The lecture is mostly about why that answer, which everyone agrees with,
fails so reliably in practice.

The mechanism is not recklessness. Continuing a marginal approach almost
always works, and each time it works, the margin that was skipped gets
retrospectively reclassified as margin that was not needed. The standard
moves. Nobody decides to lower it — it lowers through a series of
individually reasonable calls, until the accepted practice is one that
would have been rejected outright on day one.

This pattern has a literature and a name, drawn from the study of
organisational failures well outside aviation. We look at one case from a
different industry, because the shape is easier to see when the technical
detail is unfamiliar.

Then the direct part: each of you states, individually and out loud, which
gate you are going to rationalise past in week 10.
