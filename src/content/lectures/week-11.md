Week 3 argued both sides of whether a flight control system should be able
to refuse you. This lecture is the case that settles least comfortably.

There is a corner of this aircraft's envelope where it is stable in a state
you do not want: nose high, wing not flying, controls in disturbed air,
descending. The flight control system holds it there, correctly, according
to logic working exactly as specified. The input that would break it out is
one the angle-of-attack limiter exists to prevent — so the protection that
keeps you out of this state on every other day is, on this day, the reason
you cannot leave it.

Hence the override, which is the design admitting the argument has two
sides. Flip it and the limiter is gone, along with everything it was
protecting you from, and the recovery becomes available: rock the aircraft
in pitch, in phase with its own oscillation, until the nose falls through.

It costs altitude. The lecture spends its second half on how much, under
what conditions, and on the number that follows from it — the altitude
below which you stop attempting the recovery and use the seat instead.

That number is different for each of you. It is not issued by the school,
and you defend yours in the period. It is the same question asked in week
1, in the hangar, with the aircraft switched off — you have had eleven
weeks to get a better answer.
